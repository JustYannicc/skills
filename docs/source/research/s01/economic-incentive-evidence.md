# the standard evidence — incentives, trade-offs, and bounded choice

## Decision

the standard should treat incentives and friction as first-class design inputs: not as a way to pressure the operator into productivity, but as a way to make the action he has already chosen easier to start, easier to resume, and less costly to verify on a low-energy day. Every material system should make its important trade-offs inspectable, retain a humane good-enough fallback, and detect when a metric or local convenience has begun to displace the actual outcome.

This is an application of economics and decision science to one person's voluntary system. It is **not** a claim that personal preferences are stable, measurable in money, or optimisable by an agent without consent.

## Evidence table

| Concept and primary/high-trust source | What the evidence supports | the standard implication | Misuse to prevent |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Mechanism design / incentive compatibility.** Leonid Hurwicz, [“The Design of Mechanisms for Resource Allocation” (1973)](https://leonidhurwicz.org/mechanism-design-for-resource-allocation/), *AER P&P* 63(2):1–30; Nobel Committee, [Scientific Background: Mechanism Design Theory (2007)](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/advanced-economicsciences2007.pdf). | A mechanism's rules shape the information people reveal and the actions they find rational. Desirable rules must be evaluated against incentives, information, and participation—not just the designer's hoped-for outcome. | For each system, name the actor, desired outcome, information they actually have, choices offered, friction/reward/costs created, and a voluntary exit/degraded path. Ask: “On a low-energy day, does the easiest legal action still advance the operator’s stated interest?” | Treating the agent as entitled to manipulate the operator, hide options, or fabricate urgency. “Incentive compatible” here means aligned with the operator’s current, inspectable, revocable intentions—not compliance with an the agent target. |
| **Principal–agent problem / agency cost.** Jensen & Meckling, [“Theory of the Firm: Managerial Behavior, Agency Costs and Ownership Structure” (1976)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(76)90026-X), *Journal of Financial Economics* 3:305–360. | Delegation creates risk that an agent pursues its own target, proxy, convenience, or reputation rather than the principal’s objective; monitoring and bonding are costly. | Explicitly distinguish the operator’s outcome from the agent’s operational proxy (e.g., “email drafted and approved” is not “good relationship outcome”). Log the policy/version, sources, assumptions, proposal, approver, and actual effect at consequential seams. Keep authority narrow and revocable. | Assuming more automation or a higher completion rate proves alignment. It may merely show that the agent has become efficient at its own proxy. |
| **Bounded rationality, satisficing, and search cost.** Herbert A. Simon, [“A Behavioral Model of Rational Choice” (1955)](https://doi.org/10.2307/1884852), *QJE* 69:99–118; Simon, [Nobel lecture (1978)](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/simon-lecture.pdf); George Stigler, [“The Economics of Information” (1961)](https://doi.org/10.1086/258464), *JPE* 69:213–225. | Alternatives are not free: finding, understanding, and comparing them consumes scarce time and cognition. A satisfactory option found under an explicit aspiration rule can be rational when exhaustive optimisation is not. | Put a bounded recommendation first: preferred option, 1–3 materially distinct alternatives, why the recommendation clears its threshold, and what is knowingly forgone. Reuse approved defaults only within their recorded scope. Treat “search burden” as a measurable cost. | Calling any convenient answer “80/20” or “satisficing” without a stated threshold, alternatives considered, and a condition that triggers a deeper search. |
| **Opportunity cost and marginal choice.** Friedrich von Wieser, *Natural Value* (1889; English trans. 1893); see also [Mankiw, *Principles of Economics*, Ch. 3 (authoritative introductory treatment)](https://www.cengage.com/c/principles-of-economics-10e-mankiw/). | The relevant cost of a choice includes the value of the best forgone feasible alternative; marginal decisions compare incremental benefit with incremental cost, not total effort already spent. | Before custom-building, expanding scope, or demanding extra review, state a proportionate opportunity-cost note: time/attention/money/commitments displaced, expected benefit, uncertainty, reversibility, and a lower-cost adequate option. Require this only above a declared materiality threshold. | False precision (“this costs 43 minutes”) and treating opportunity cost as a veto. It is a surfaced trade-off for the operator’s decision, not a productivity score. |
| **Sunk-cost fallacy.** Hal Arkes & Peter Ayton, [“The Sunk Cost and Concorde Effects” (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0749-5978(99)00011-9), *OBHDP* 79:591–602; Hal Arkes & Catherine Blumer, [“The Psychology of Sunk Cost” (1985)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-5978(85)90049-4). | Past, unrecoverable investment can distort continuation decisions; it should be separated from prospective value and prospective cost. | Change/pause/retire reviews must show: sunk investment (context only), future benefit if continued, remaining cost, exit cost, reversible experiment, and stop condition. | Using “sunk cost” to dismiss legitimate commitments, learning value, reputational effects, safety requirements, or a decision to finish because it remains worthwhile. |
| **Pareto / 80–20.** Vilfredo Pareto, *Cours d’économie politique* (1896–97); Joseph M. Juran, *Managerial Breakthrough* (1964), ch. 4 (“the vital few and useful many”). | Pareto documented a skewed wealth distribution; Juran adapted the label to quality management. Neither establishes a universal 80/20 ratio nor identifies the high-leverage actions in a new domain. | Use “80/20” only as a hypothesis for triage: inspect whether a small set of causes/actions accounts for most material outcome, then test locally. Record the observed distribution, population/window, and excluded harms. | Treating 80/20 as a law, dropping “the last 20%” when it contains safety, recovery, accessibility, legal, trust, or rare catastrophic cases. |
| **Goodhart and Campbell effects.** Charles Goodhart, [“Problems of Monetary Management” (1975)](https://www.goodhart.com/), in *Papers in Monetary Economics*; Donald T. Campbell, [“Assessing the Impact of Planned Social Change” (1976)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED178584.pdf). | Measures used as targets are vulnerable to distortion and can corrupt the process they are meant to monitor, particularly when stakes are high. | A metric must name its decision, outcome proxy, failure mode, counter-signal, collection burden, owner, and retirement/review date. No metric alone authorises wider autonomy or declares success. Sample outcome quality and harm, not merely throughput. | Converting personal measurement into a quota, score of worth, or surveillance system. Never reward the agent for a proxy it can manipulate (e.g., number of tasks closed). |
| **Loss aversion / reference dependence.** Daniel Kahneman & Amos Tversky, [“Prospect Theory” (1979)](https://doi.org/10.2307/1914185), *Econometrica* 47:263–291; Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler, [“Experimental Tests of the Endowment Effect” (1990)](https://doi.org/10.1086/261737), *JPE* 98:1325–1348. | Choices are often reference-dependent; prospective losses can weigh more than equivalent gains. This is behavioral decision theory, not a licence to exploit fear. | Frame maintenance and recovery truthfully: identify what a delayed action risks losing (deadline, money, relationship, accumulated friction) beside the upside and the safe degraded path. Offer opt-in commitments only where the operator explicitly chooses them while regulated. | Fear-based nudges, streak penalties, shame, artificial scarcity, or irreversible “commitment devices” imposed by the agent. These can create avoidance, distrust, and proxy gaming. |
| **Transaction and switching costs.** Ronald Coase, [“The Nature of the Firm” (1937)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0335.1937.tb00002.x), *Economica* 4:386–405; Paul Klemperer, [“Markets with Consumer Switching Costs” (1987)](https://doi.org/10.2307/1885068), *QJE* 102:375–394. | Coordination, discovery, negotiation, setup, and switching impose real costs; the least theoretically optimal process may be best if its transition cost overwhelms its advantage. | Treat “cost of search” and “cost to resume/switch” as design risks. Preserve context, provide a resumption pointer, minimize required handoffs, and use stable handoff contracts. Before replacing a tool/system, compare transition, training, migration, rollback, and lock-in costs. | Equating familiar with good, or using switching costs to protect a failing system. A high switching cost requires an explicit transition plan, not indefinite stagnation. |
| **Irreversibility and real options.** Avinash Dixit & Robert Pindyck, *Investment under Uncertainty* (1994), [Princeton University Press](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691034102/investment-under-uncertainty). | Under uncertainty, deferring an irreversible commitment can have option value because waiting may reveal information. | Classify consequential decisions as reversible, costly-to-reverse, or irreversible. Prefer a time-bounded pilot/simulation when it cheaply produces decision-relevant information; define the cost of delay as well as the value of waiting. | Perpetual deferral disguised as optionality. Give every pilot a learning question, time/effort cap, evidence threshold, and explicit retain/revise/stop decision. |

## Quantitative fields worth capturing

Capture only fields attached to a decision. The defaults below are event facts, not self-ratings.

| Decision | Minimal quantitative fields | Required qualitative/evidence companion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is the intake/handoff reducing search and resumption cost? | captured timestamp; first-touch timestamp; resolved/abandoned timestamp; number of clarification cycles; number of handoffs; resumption age | reason for delay/abandonment; whether the outcome still mattered |
| Is an approved email/action system accurate and low burden? | proposals; approvals; edits before approval; corrections after effect; time-to-approval; time-to-correction; replay pass rate | what was misunderstood; governing source/rule corrected |
| Is a degraded path actually recoverable? | missed-briefing interval; preserved items; resumed items; time-to-first-safe-next-action; stale backlog age | why recovery failed; what contextual or physical barrier mattered |
| Should a tool/system change continue? | baseline and post-change outcome count/rate; active minutes/steps where passively observable; error/rework count; migration/maintenance time; money spent | benefit/harm examples; confounders; whether the outcome definition still fits |
| Is a metric being gamed or becoming harmful? | proxy trend alongside outcome/counter-signal; distribution across normal/low-energy periods; exceptions; opt-outs; manual overrides | examples of proxy improvement with outcome worsening; pressure/distrust/avoidance evidence |

Do not infer mood, motivation, health, productivity, or causal treatment effect from sparse operational events. Health/biometric data requires a purpose, consent, data-minimisation, retention/access rule, and a non-medical interpretation boundary; the agent must not make clinical claims from it.

## Precise the standard clause recommendations

1. **Universal entry check — add “intent and alternatives” after Outcome.**

   > **Intent and alternatives:** What underlying need, constraint, or avoided loss is the operator trying to address? Is the requested action the best currently available means, or is there an existing/adequate lower-cost path? Record the answer, competing interpretations, and any assumption that materially changes the recommendation.

   The check is not an interrogation gate. For a reversible direct request, the agent may state its tentative interpretation and proceed to a draft. For a material external effect, system change, or unresolved alternative that changes outcome/cost/commitment, it creates a clarification item or presents a bounded recommendation before effect.

2. **Constitutional rule 1 — replace with an outcome-and-trade-off rule.**

   > **Outcome, intent, and trade-off before tool.** Before selecting or configuring a solution, state the desired human outcome, likely underlying need where material, affected actors, constraints, non-goals, viable existing/adequate alternatives, and the material trade-off of the recommended path. Prefer a reversible, good-enough existing solution when it clears the stated threshold; custom-build only when its expected incremental value justifies its opportunity, transition, and maintenance costs.

3. **Constitutional rule 2 — rename to lowest-common-denominator operation and add incentive alignment.**

   > **Designed for the lowest-common-denominator real state.** The system must preserve essentials on low-energy, low-initiation, interrupted, or dysregulated days. Its lowest-friction permitted action must advance the operator’s stated outcome or safely preserve the option to do so; it must not reward proxy completion, conceal cost, or impose shame/penalty. The minimum path is a floor, not a ceiling.

4. **Add a constitutional rule immediately after measurement.**

   > **Incentives and costs are inspectable.** Every material system identifies the incentives, friction, search/transaction costs, switching costs, and proxies it creates for the operator, the agent, and other material actors. It records foreseeable misalignment or gaming routes, the counter-signal or control that detects them, and the voluntary degraded/exit path. the agent may make a preferred action easier, but may not create coercive pressure or treat compliance as evidence of wellbeing.

5. **System Record — add a “Decision economics” field under Frame/Contract when material.**

   `decision; underlying intent; options considered; adequacy threshold; recommendation and forgone alternative; expected benefit; opportunity/transition/maintenance cost; reversibility; cost of delay; incentive map; gaming/misalignment route; counter-signal; voluntary exit/degraded path.`

6. **Measurement section — preserve quantitative priority without false precision.** Replace “Quantitative data is optional when it would create false precision” with:

   > Quantitative operational data is preferred whenever it can be collected proportionately and answer the decision question. Qualitative evidence remains required for meaning, harm, and exceptions that counts cannot validly represent. Do not invent numerical precision, use sparse data to claim causality, or retain telemetry without a named decision, owner, and retirement rule.

7. **Change / anti-decay — require forward-looking continuation decisions.** Add: `A retain, expand, pause, replace, or retire decision separates unrecoverable past investment from prospective benefit, remaining cost, exit cost, reversibility, and the cost of delay. A maintenance reset must record the new baseline/date from which decay is assessed.`

8. **Acceptance scenarios — add two cases.**

   - **Incentive failure:** A design raises its proxy (e.g., tasks closed) by making low-value work easiest. The counter-signal exposes the divergence; the agent proposes an incentive/friction correction and does not promote the design.
   - **Good-enough alternative:** the operator requests a custom solution. the agent compares a bounded existing workaround, custom build, and defer option with adequacy threshold, opportunity/transition cost, reversibility, and cost of delay; the operator can choose without hidden optimisation.

## Source notes

- “80/20” should be written as a **heuristic to investigate concentration**, never a quantified prediction. Pareto's original work concerns income/wealth distributions; Juran’s management adaptation does not prove the ratio applies to the agent work.
- Goodhart’s original statement concerns monetary targets. Campbell provides the broader high-stakes measurement warning. The the standard application is an inference and needs local evidence.
- Prospect theory is useful for explaining why loss framing can be salient. It does not establish that the agent should use loss framing, and the autonomy constraint is normative rather than a result from the paper.
